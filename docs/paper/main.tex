\title{Skill, luck and risk in bits: scoring guesses in feedback-guessing games}
\author{The Lexodle project}
\date{Working draft, \today}

\begin{abstract}
In a Wordle-like game every guess is a question and the feedback is the answer. We
score a guess by the information it gains, in bits. The expected gain $H$ is the
Shannon entropy of the way the guess splits the words still possible; \emph{skill}
is $H$ as a share of the best real guess's. \emph{Luck} is where the gain you got
falls among the gains the guess could have given, a mid-distribution percentile
with mean zero. \emph{Risk} is the sign and size of the skew of that distribution
about its mean: how far a guess leaned on a rare large gain. Gains add over a game
to the bits needed to find the word, so a game's bits split exactly into an
expected part and a luck part that is a martingale. A player's habits are read from
which of five behaviours each guess is; six playstyles are the pairs of an early and
an end habit. The definitions, their properties, the handling of the last try and of
several boards, and simulations of the playstyles are given. The maths was written
with an AI assistant and has not been peer reviewed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

In Wordle-like games the player guesses a word. Each letter is marked as in the
right place, elsewhere in the word, or absent. After the game two questions arise.
Skill: given what was known, how good was the guess? Luck: given the guess, how
favourable was the feedback? A score that mixes the two is uninformative.

Entropy-based solvers choose the guess that maximises the expected information
in the feedback \cite{shannon1948,sanderson2022}, as for the code game
Mastermind \cite{knuth1977,kooi2005}. Exact methods give optimal strategies for the
original word list \cite{bertsimas2022}; for generalised lists, deciding whether a
win within a given number of guesses can be guaranteed is NP-hard
\cite{lokshtanov2022}. A newspaper review tool reports skill and luck per guess
\cite{nyt2022}; we know of no published formula. This paper does not address how to
play. It defines scores to show a player after the game. The requirements are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item[(R1)] \textbf{Knowledge only.} Skill and risk do not depend on the answer.
Luck depends on the answer only through the feedback the player saw.
\item[(R2)] \textbf{Bounded.} Skill runs from $0$ (the guess tells nothing) to $100$
(as good as any real guess). Luck is bounded and symmetric about $0$. Risk runs from
$0$ to $100$.
\item[(R3)] \textbf{Fair luck.} Under the law the answer is drawn from, luck has mean
zero given the history and is uncorrelated with the skill of the same and earlier
guesses (Section~\ref{sec:luck}).
\item[(R4)] \textbf{One plot.} All three can be read from one plot of the outcomes
the guess could have had (Section~\ref{sec:plot}).
\item[(R5)] \textbf{Accounting.} The bits gained over a game equal the bits expected
plus a luck term, exactly, and add up to the bits needed (Section~\ref{sec:bits}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Setting}\label{sec:setting}

A game has a finite list of words, and the hidden word is drawn by a \emph{law}:
each word $w$ has a chance $p_w$ (equal for every word, or in proportion to how
common the word is). Let $P$ be the set of words still possible, with total chance
$p(P)=\sum_{w\in P}p_w$. A guess $g$ gives each word $w\in P$ a feedback pattern, and
so splits $P$ into groups $G_1,\dots,G_m$ by pattern. The \emph{chance} of group $j$
is $q_j=p(G_j)/p(P)$, the chance, given what is known, that the answer is in it. The
guess is \emph{real} if the game accepts it as a word; it need not be a possible
answer. When the law is equal, $q_j=|G_j|/|P|$.

\section{Gain and skill}\label{sec:skill}

\begin{definition}
Landing in group $j$ \emph{gains} $\Gain_j=-\log_2 q_j$ bits. The \emph{expected
gain} of $g$ is
\[
H(g)=\sum_j q_j\,\Gain_j=-\sum_j q_j\log_2 q_j .
\]
\end{definition}

A bit halves the chance mass still possible: a gain of $\Gain_j$ bits leaves a
share $2^{-\Gain_j}$ of the chance. $H(g)$ is the Shannon entropy \cite{shannon1948}
of the split. It is $0$ exactly when one group holds all the chance (the guess tells
nothing), at most $\log_2 m$, and, for equal chances, at most $\log_2|P|$.

\begin{proposition}[Refinement]\label{prop:refine}
If a guess $g'$ splits every group of $g$ further, then $H(g')\ge H(g)$, with
equality only if no group is actually divided.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Write a group $G_j$ of $g$ as groups of $g'$ with chances $q_{jk}$, $\sum_k q_{jk}=q_j$.
Then $H(g')-H(g)=\sum_j q_j\,H(q_{j1}/q_j,\dots)\ge0$, the entropy of the division of
each group, which is $0$ only for an undivided group.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
Let $H^*$ be the largest $H(g)$ over the real guesses scored. The \emph{skill} of
$g$ is $100\,H(g)/H^*$ (and $100$ if $H^*=0$, when no guess tells anything).
\end{definition}

Skill depends on $P$, the law and the guess, never on the answer (R1), and lies in
$[0,100]$ with $100$ for the best real guess. The scale is straight in bits: a guess
that gains half the bits of the best scores 50. The app scores every word the game
accepts; $H^*$ is found by scanning them.

\section{Luck}\label{sec:luck}

Let $\Gain$ be the gain of a guess when the answer is drawn by the law: it takes the
value $\Gain_j$ with chance $q_j$.

\begin{definition}
The \emph{luck} of landing in group $j$ is
\[
\Luck_j=\Prob(\Gain<\Gain_j)-\Prob(\Gain>\Gain_j)\in(-1,1),
\]
shown $\times100$. It is $2F_{\mathrm{mid}}(\Gain_j)-1$, where $F_{\mathrm{mid}}$ is the
mid-distribution function \cite{lancaster1961,parzen2004}: the chance of a smaller gain
plus half the chance of an equal one.
\end{definition}

A larger gain is a luckier outcome. In terms of chances, $\Prob(\Gain<\Gain_j)$ is
the chance mass of the groups more likely than $j$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:luck}
Under the law, $\E[\Luck]=0$ and $\Var(\Luck)=(1-\sum_j q_j^3)/3$. The luck is
strictly between $-1$ and $1$ and equals $0$ for every outcome of a guess with one
group.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
$F_{\mathrm{mid}}(\Gain)$ has the distribution of a continuous uniform variable
spread over each atom: its mean is $1/2$ and its variance $\tfrac1{12}(1-\sum q_j^3)$,
so $2F_{\mathrm{mid}}-1$ has mean $0$ and variance $(1-\sum q_j^3)/3$. Ties give the
same luck. One group gives $\Prob(\Gain<\Gain_1)=\Prob(\Gain>\Gain_1)=0$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Luck in bits.} The difference $\lambda_j=\Gain_j-H(g)$ is the gain above
the expected gain. Its mean is $0$ exactly, and it is what the accounting of
Section~\ref{sec:bits} sums. The percentile $\Luck$ is its bounded, scale-free
version: $\Luck$ and $\lambda$ have the same sign in the sense that $\Luck_j$ is
increasing in $\Gain_j$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:uncorr}
Over a game, the luck in bits of successive guesses are martingale differences with
respect to the feedback so far; so they are uncorrelated with each other, and with the
skill of any guess, which is known before the feedback.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Given the history, the pool and the law of the next feedback are fixed and
$\E[\lambda_t\mid\text{history}]=0$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:luck}'s argument applied to
$\Gain-H$. For $s<t$, $\E[\lambda_s\lambda_t]=\E[\lambda_s\E[\lambda_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]]=0$,
and skill is a function of the history, so $\E[\mathrm{skill}_t\lambda_t]=0$.
\end{proof}

The same holds for the percentile $\Luck$, since it too has mean zero given the
history; the correlation of the percentiles of different guesses is zero by the same
argument.

\paragraph{Under the wrong law.} If a game scored luck with equal chances while the
answer was drawn by frequency, the mean would no longer be zero. The app scores every
game under the law it was drawn by (equal for a daily).

\section{Risk}\label{sec:risk}

Some guesses gain nearly the same whatever the feedback; others usually gain a little
and now and then a lot. Risk is the second kind.

\begin{definition}
The \emph{raw risk} of $g$ is
$D(g)=\Prob(\Gain<H)-\Prob(\Gain>H)\in(-1,1)$: the chance of gaining less than
expected less the chance of gaining more. Let $D^*$ be the largest $D$ over the real
guesses scored. The \emph{risk} of $g$ is $100\max(0,D(g))/D^*$, and $0$ if
$D^*\le0$.
\end{definition}

$D>0$ means the gain is skewed to the right, the shape of a gamble: a likely small
gain, an unlikely large one (mean above median). A guess with equal groups has
$D=0$. Risk is a property of the guess, not of the outcome, so it is known before the
feedback and does not move with luck. It is shown beside luck in the review: the
chevrons by the risk say whether the outcome landed on the rare large gain or not.

\section{Bits over a game}\label{sec:bits}

Let guess $t$ land in a group of chance $q_t$ in the pool $P_{t-1}$, leaving
$P_t$.

\begin{proposition}[Chain rule]\label{prop:chain}
$\sum_{t\le T}\Gain_t=-\log_2 p(P_T)/p(P_0)$. With the law normalised,
$\sum_t\Gain_t=-\log_2 p(P_T)$, which is at most $-\log_2 p_a$ for the answer $a$,
with equality when $P_T=\{a\}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$q_t=p(P_t)/p(P_{t-1})$, so $\prod_t q_t=p(P_T)/p(P_0)$, and $a\in P_T$.
\end{proof}

The bits needed to find the word are $-\log_2 p_a$: $\log_2 N$ for equal chances.
A guess that wins counts as cutting all the remaining bits. Then, with
$\lambda_t=\Gain_t-H_t$,
\[
\sum_t\Gain_t=\sum_t H_t+\sum_t\lambda_t ,
\]
the bits expected (the skill part) plus the luck part, a sum of martingale
differences with mean zero (Theorem~\ref{thm:uncorr}). This is the exact accounting
(R5). The review draws it as a waterfall, one bar for each guess, racing to the
bits needed.

\section{The last try}\label{sec:last}

On the last try nothing is left to learn: only a guess that could be the answer can
win. Skill is then the chance the guess wins, as a share of the best chance among
the real guesses (the likeliest word); a guess that cannot be the answer scores $0$.
Luck is the outcome less the chance: $1-c$ for a win, $-c$ for a miss, where $c$ is
the chance of winning; its mean is zero. With one word left, a guess is the word
(skill 100, gain $0$ bits, risk $0$, luck $0$, words $1\to0$) or a miss (skill $0$).
Risk is not defined on the last try, nor with one word left.

\section{One plot}\label{sec:plot}

Draw one column for each possible outcome of the guess, as wide as its chance $q_j$ and
as tall as its gain $\Gain_j$, the outcome that gains least on the left. The area under
the columns is $H$; a dashed line at the mean height marks $H$, a dotted one the best
guess's $H^*$ (skill is the first over the second). The columns taller than the mean
are the right tail a risky guess gambles on. A line splits the width in half: the
column it crosses has luck $0$, and the column landed in is outlined: its distance from
the middle is its luck. Skill, risk and luck are then read from one picture (R4).

\section{Several boards}\label{sec:multi}

In a quartet, one guess serves four boards; in a relay race it serves the board that
is on. Each board is scored as a game of its own: its $H$, its $H^*$, its luck. A
quartet guess has a skill and a luck that are the means over the unsolved boards
weighted by the bits still to find on each, $\log_2$ of its words left (at least one
bit), so a board with one word left counts for one bit and playing that word is
rewarded. Bits add over boards: the gain of a step is the sum of its gains. In a relay
race, a board opened later starts with every earlier guess applied; the review reports
how many words were left then (its head start).

\section{Playstyles}\label{sec:styles}

A player's style is read from their last 100 classic games, a daily being one. A guess
is counted in a phase by the words left $n$ and the greens known: \emph{early} for
$n\ge64$, \emph{end} for $n\le16$ or three greens, otherwise the middle, which is not
read. Each early guess is counted as the first that applies of:

\begin{itemize}
\item a \textbf{gambler}: risk $\ge75$;
\item a \textbf{maximiser}: in the top $10\%$ of the guesses scored, by expected gain;
\item an \textbf{eliminator}: four or more letters never tried in the game.
\end{itemize}

and each end guess as the first of:

\begin{itemize}
\item a \textbf{closer}: the guess fits every clue, so it can win;
\item a \textbf{prober}: it does not fit, ranks in the top $10\%$, and three or more
words are left.
\end{itemize}

A \emph{habit} is the behaviour with the largest share of a phase's guesses, if that
share is at least $40\%$. A player is rated once there are ten games and ten guesses in
each phase; a habit already held is kept while its share is within $0.05$ of the
leader's. The \emph{style} is the pair, early habit and end habit:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
 & prober at the end & closer at the end\\
\midrule
maximiser early & analyst & maverick\\
eliminator early & detective & sweeper\\
gambler early & wildcard & gambler\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Simulated players.} Six policies play the real list (English, five letters,
3,000 answers, equal chances), each following its style (the gamblers take their risks
on the first two turns and then settle). Over 30 games each, judged game by game
with the rules above, the style is read back as follows (script:
\texttt{experiments/styles/sim2.js}):

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
policy & read as itself & end habit unread & another style\\
\midrule
analyst & 20 & 5 & 5\\
maverick & 25 & 5 & 0\\
detective & 18 & 3 & 9\\
sweeper & 24 & 3 & 3\\
wildcard & 16 & 8 & 6\\
gambler & 26 & 4 & 0\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A single game has few end guesses, so its end habit is often unread; pooled over the
games a player has (the app needs ten) the habits settle. The thresholds come from
these simulations, not from measured people, and are the first thing to revisit with
real play.

\section{Computation}\label{sec:computation}

For a pool of $n$ words and $c$ candidate guesses, $H$ and $D$ of every candidate take
$O(nc)$ pattern evaluations, grouped by a pattern code; $H^*$ and $D^*$ are maxima over
them. The app scans every word the game accepts, in the background while the game is
played, one guess after another. When two words are left it scans only those two.

\section{Open problems}\label{sec:open}

\begin{itemize}
\item Calibrate the playstyle thresholds on real games.
\item Is there a closed form for the best achievable $H^*$ for a given pool, beyond
the bound $\log_2 m$?
\item A skill that rewards the guesses that set up later splits (a two-step $H$).
\item Whether risk predicts the tries a game takes once skill and luck are known.
\end{itemize}

\section{Reproducibility}\label{sec:repro}

The definitions are implemented in \texttt{app/lib/logic.dart} (gain, $H$, $D$, skill,
luck, risk, the landscape of all guesses), \texttt{app/lib/playstyle.dart} (the
behaviours and styles), \texttt{app/lib/review\_plots.dart} (the plot and the waterfall)
and \texttt{app/lib/multi\_analysis.dart} (several boards), and tested in
\texttt{app/test}. The playstyle simulation is
\texttt{docs/paper/experiments/styles}. The scripts of the earlier,
pool-size version of the maths (\texttt{experiments/*.py}) are kept for the record;
they measure the retired expected-pool skill and the retired daring and tries
regression, and no claim above rests on them.
