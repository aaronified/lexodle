\begin{abstract}
We define skill, risk and luck for a guess in a word game with colour feedback
(Wordle and variants). The answer is drawn by a known \emph{law}: every word equally
likely, or each by its weight (word frequency). A guess partitions the words still
possible by the feedback they would give. The answer lies in one class; the
\emph{pool} is the class's share of the law, expressed as a number of equally likely
words (its size, under equal chances).
\emph{Skill} is the log-reduction of the expected pool, as a fraction of the same
quantity for the best real guess. \emph{Luck} is the probability of a larger pool
minus the probability of a smaller one. It lies strictly in $(-100,100)$ percent.
Under the law the answer is drawn from, and only under that law, it has mean zero for
every guess, and its increments over a game are martingale differences, so luck is
uncorrelated with the skill of the same and earlier guesses (not independent of it).
The two simpler luck definitions are unbounded or biased. The log-reduction of the
candidate set equals skill plus risk plus luck exactly, where risk is the gap between
Shannon and collision entropy. On the last try skill is the guess's chance of winning
as a share of the best chance any guess has, and luck is the outcome less that chance.
\emph{Daring} measures how far the player went for the whole word instead of the clues:
it is zero for a guess that cannot be the answer, and otherwise grows with what the
attempt cost in skill, how unlikely the word was, and how many tries were left. At the
opening guess, where every word is possible, it is a function of skill alone (and
the word's chance). We extend the definitions to games with
several boards, give an exact algorithm, and report experiments on an English
five-letter list ($N=4{,}994$) and simulated players of several habits.
The best first guess leaves $114.4$ words on average (equal chances).
\textsc{crane} scores
$81.6$ ($97.6$ on a linear scale, which puts most openings near $100$). A bound on
the best conceivable split from the number of feedback patterns is too loose to
use. We list open problems.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

In Wordle-like games the player guesses a word. Each letter is marked as in the
right place, elsewhere in the word, or absent. After the game two questions arise.
Skill: given what was known, how good was the guess? Luck: given the guess, how
favourable was the feedback? A score that mixes the two is uninformative.

Entropy-based solvers choose the guess that maximises the expected information
in the feedback \cite{shannon1948,sanderson2022}, as for the code game
Mastermind \cite{knuth1977,kooi2005}. Exact methods give optimal strategies for the
original word list \cite{bertsimas2022}. For generalised word lists, deciding
whether a win within a given number of guesses can be guaranteed is NP-hard
\cite{lokshtanov2022}. A newspaper review tool reports skill and luck per guess
\cite{nyt2022}; we know of no published formula. This paper does not address how to
play. It defines scores to show a player after the game. The requirements are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item[(R1)] \textbf{Knowledge only.} Neither score depends on the answer except
through what the player saw (and the law the game draws answers by, which is a rule
of the game).
\item[(R2)] \textbf{Bounded.} Skill runs from $0$ (the guess tells nothing) to $100$
(as good as any real guess). Luck is symmetric about $0$ and bounded.
\item[(R3)] \textbf{Fair luck.} Under the law the answer is drawn from, luck has mean
zero (given the history) and is uncorrelated with the skill of the same and earlier
guesses. It is unbiased only with respect to that law (Section~\ref{sec:laws}).
\item[(R4)] \textbf{One plot.} Both scores can be read from a single plot of the
outcomes the guess could have had.
\item[(R5)] \textbf{Accounting.} The reduction of the candidate set equals a skill
term plus a risk term plus a luck term, exactly (Section~\ref{sec:accounting}).
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:setting} fixes the setting, Section~\ref{sec:skill} defines skill,
Section~\ref{sec:luck} luck, Section~\ref{sec:accounting} the accounting,
Section~\ref{sec:daring} daring,
Section~\ref{sec:multi} several boards, Section~\ref{sec:computation} the algorithm,
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} the experiments, Section~\ref{sec:open} the open
problems. Code and scripts are listed in Section~\ref{sec:repro}.

\section{Setting}\label{sec:setting}

Notation. $\ell$ is the word length, $N=|\Omega|$ the number of words still possible,
$W$ the (random) answer, $\Pool$ the pool a guess leaves (a number of words, possibly
effective, see below), $S\in[0,1]$ a skill and $D\in[0,1)$ a daring (both quoted as
percentages), $\Luck\in(-1,1)$ a luck (quoted as a percentage).

Let $\Omega$ be the finite set of words still possible given everything the player
has seen, $N=|\Omega|\ge1$, and $\mathcal G$ the finite set of admissible guesses.
Assume $\Omega\subseteq\mathcal G$. Let $\Phi$ be the set of feedback patterns
($\{\text{grey},\text{yellow},\text{green}\}^\ell$ for $\ell$-letter words) and
$f\colon\mathcal G\times\Omega\to\Phi$ the feedback function. Only two properties of
$f$ are used: it is computable, and $f(g,w)$ is all green if and only if $g=w$.

\subsection{Answer laws}\label{sec:laws}

\begin{definition}[Answer law]
A law is a weight $\pi(\omega)>0$ for each $\omega\in\Omega$, $M=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\pi(\omega)$.
The answer $W$ has $\Prob(W=\omega)=\pi(\omega)/M$. Feedback is a deterministic
function of the answer and the guesses, so given the history of guesses and
feedback, $W$ has the law $\pi$ restricted to the words consistent with the history,
and $M$ is replaced by the weight of those words.
\end{definition}

Three laws are used. \textbf{(U)} Every possible word equally likely, $\pi\equiv1$
(frequency off). \textbf{(F)} Each word by its weight, its frequency in the word list
(frequency on). \textbf{(D)} The daily: uniform over the $3{,}000$ most common words
and zero elsewhere. A word with zero chance is not possible, so $\Omega$ is those
$3{,}000$ words and (D) is (U) on a smaller $\Omega$; the other words can be played
as probes ($\mathcal G\supsetneq\Omega$). This is how \texttt{predict\_tries3.dart}
treats it. Every score below is relative to one law, which is part of the rules of
the game and not of the answer, so (R1) holds. What each score is relative to is
stated in Theorem~\ref{thm:luck} and Section~\ref{sec:wronglaw}.

A guess $g\in\mathcal G$ partitions $\Omega$ into nonempty classes
$C_\varphi=\{w\in\Omega: f(g,w)=\varphi\}$, $k$ of them. Let $\mu_i=\pi(C_i)$ be the mass
of class $i$ and $q_i=\mu_i/M$ the chance that the answer lies in it, $\sum_iq_i=1$.
The \emph{effective size} of the class is $s_i=Nq_i$, so $\sum_is_i=N$; under (U)
$s_i=|C_i|$. The answer lands in one class. Its effective size $\Pool_g=s_{i(W)}$ is the
\emph{pool}: the number of equally likely words that would be as hard to tell apart.
Under (U) it is the number of words still possible after the guess. If $\nu_x$ classes
have effective size $x$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pool-law}
\Prob(\Pool_g=x)=\frac{x\,\nu_x}{N}.
\end{equation}
Pools are size-biased: a large class is more likely to contain the answer. The
definitions of skill and luck, Lemma~\ref{lem:E}, Proposition~\ref{prop:skill} and
Theorems~\ref{thm:luck} and~\ref{thm:martingale} use only $N$, the effective sizes and their
order, and hold for every law with sizes read as effective sizes (which need not be
integers). Statements that count words, such as the examples of
Proposition~\ref{prop:alternatives}, are for (U).

\section{Skill}\label{sec:skill}

\begin{definition}[Expected pool]
$E(g)=\E[\Pool_g]=\dfrac1N\sum_{i=1}^k s_i^2=N\sum_{i=1}^kq_i^2$.
\end{definition}

Under (U) this is $\sum_i|C_i|^2/N$. In general $\sum_iq_i^2$ is the chance that two
independent answers fall in the same class, and $E$ is $N$ times it.

\begin{definition}[Best real guess, skill]
$E^\ast=\min_{g\in\mathcal G}E(g)$. For $N\ge2$ the \emph{skill} of $g\in\mathcal G$ is
\[
S(g)=\frac{\ln\bigl(N/E(g)\bigr)}{\ln\bigl(N/E^\ast\bigr)}\in[0,1],
\]
reported as a percentage. For $N=1$, $S(g)=1$ if $g$ is the remaining word and $0$
otherwise.
\end{definition}

The logarithm is used because a guess cuts the candidate set by a factor. On the linear
scale $(N-E)/(N-E^\ast)$ most sensible guesses score near $100$
(Section~\ref{sec:experiments}). The linear score has the same endpoints and the same
ordering of guesses.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:E}
Let the partition of $g$ have $k$ classes, and let $E_{\min}=N\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}(\pi(\omega)/M)^2$
(the value of $E$ for the partition into singletons).
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\max\{N/k,E_{\min}\}\le E(g)\le N$; $E(g)=N$ iff $k=1$; $E(g)=E_{\min}$ iff
$k=N$; $E(g)=N/k$ iff all classes have equal mass. Also $E_{\min}\ge1$, with equality
iff $\pi$ is constant. Under (U), $E(g)=1$ iff $k=N$.
\item If the partition of $g'$ properly refines that of $g$, then $E(g')<E(g)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
(1) $q_i\le1$ gives $\sum q_i^2\le\sum q_i=1$, with equality iff $k=1$, so $E\le N$.
By Cauchy--Schwarz $\sum q_i^2\ge(\sum q_i)^2/k=1/k$, with equality iff the $q_i$ are
equal. A class of chance $q_i$ is a sum of the chances $p_\omega=\pi(\omega)/M$ of its
words, and $(\sum p_\omega)^2\ge\sum p_\omega^2$, with equality iff the class has one
word (the weights are positive); so $E\ge E_{\min}$, with equality iff $k=N$. Finally
$\sum p_\omega^2\ge1/N$ by Cauchy--Schwarz, with equality iff the $p_\omega$ are equal.
(2) Splitting a class of chance $b_1+\dots+b_r$, $r\ge2$, replaces $(\sum b_j)^2$ by
$\sum b_j^2$, which is smaller because the cross terms are positive.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:skill}
Let $N\ge2$ and $\Omega\subseteq\mathcal G$. Then $E^\ast<N$, so $S$ is defined, and
\begin{enumerate}
\item $0\le S(g)\le1$ for every $g\in\mathcal G$; $S(g)=1$ iff $E(g)=E^\ast$;
$S(g)=0$ iff $g$ does not divide $\Omega$;
\item $S$ is strictly decreasing in $E(g)$ and affine in $\ln E(g)$: halving the
expected pool adds $\ln2/\ln(N/E^\ast)$. If the partition of $g'$ properly refines
that of $g$, then $S(g')>S(g)$;
\item $S(g)$ is a function of $(\Omega,\pi,g,\mathcal G)$ alone. It does not depend on
the answer.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Take $w\in\Omega\subseteq\mathcal G$. The guess $w$ gives all green against $w$, and
against no other word (all green iff $g=w$), so $\{w\}$ is a class of $w$'s partition. For $N\ge2$ the partition is nontrivial, so
$E(w)<N$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:E} and $E^\ast<N$. The denominator is positive.
(1) follows from $E^\ast\le E(g)\le N$ and the lemma, (2) from the formula and
Lemma~\ref{lem:E}(2), (3) because $E(g)$ and $E^\ast$ are computed from the classes of
$\Omega$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Entropy]
Then $\ln(N/E)=-\ln\sum q_i^2=H_2$, the R\'enyi entropy of order $2$
\cite{renyi1961} of the split chances; $e^{H_2}$ is the inverse Simpson index, the Hill
number of order $2$
\cite{simpson1949,hill1973}. Hence $S(g)=H_2(g)/\max_{g'}H_2(g')$: the collision-entropy
share of the best guess. The Shannon
entropy $H=-\sum q_i\ln q_i=\ln N-\E[\ln\Pool_g]$ \cite{shannon1948} is the expected
log-reduction of the candidate set, and $H\ge H_2$ by Jensen. $H_2$ is used because it
is a function of $E$, the expected number of words left. Other orders of R\'enyi entropy
give other scales (Section~\ref{sec:risk}).
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Why the best real guess]
A bound that needs no search is $e=N/\min(N,3^\ell)$, the most even split into
$\min(N,3^\ell)$ groups (a lower bound on $E$ under every law, by Lemma~\ref{lem:E}(1)).
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} tests it and two bounds that use the clues; they never
improve on it, and it is too loose. The best real word makes about two thirds of the
groups the bound allows when many words remain, and rarely separates every word when few
remain. $E^\ast$ is therefore the actual minimum over real guesses. The floor of $E^\ast$
is $E_{\min}$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:E}), reached only if some guess separates every word.
\end{remark}

\section{Luck}\label{sec:luck}

Let $q_x=\Prob(\Pool_g=x)=x\,\nu_x/N$. Pools are ordered by effective size, that is by
the chance of their class: a class is bigger when it is more likely to hold the answer.

\begin{definition}[Luck]
For $x$ in the support of $\Pool=\Pool_g$,
\[
\Luck_g(x)=\Prob(\Pool>x)-\Prob(\Pool<x).
\]
The luck of an outcome is $\Luck_g(\Pool_g)$, reported as a percentage.
\end{definition}

It is the probability of a larger pool minus the probability of a smaller one: negative
if the outcome is worse than most, positive if better, zero at the median.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:luck}
Let the answer be drawn by the law $\pi$ used to compute the effective sizes, let
$\Pool$ have the law \eqref{eq:pool-law} and $\Luck=\Luck_g$. (Only the law of
$\Pool$ is used in the proof; (U) is the case $s_i=|C_i|$.)
\begin{enumerate}
\item (Mid-distribution form.) $\Luck(x)=2\tilde F(x)-1$ with
$\tilde F(x)=\Prob(\Pool>x)+\tfrac12\Prob(\Pool=x)$. $\Luck(\Pool)$ is Cliff's delta
between the realised pool and an independent copy \cite{cliff1993}.
\item (Bounded.) $-1<\Luck(x)<1$ for every $x$ in the support, and $\Luck$ is strictly
decreasing in $x$.
\item (Fair.) $\E[\Luck(\Pool)]=0$ for every guess, when the answer follows the law $\pi$.
\item (Spread.) $\Var\,\Luck(\Pool)=\dfrac{1-\sum_x q_x^3}{3}<\dfrac13$, the sum over the
distinct pool sizes $x$ (equal pools are one atom: $q_x$ is the chance of landing in any
class of effective size $x$, so ties lower the variance).
\item (Invariance.) $\Luck(x)$ is unchanged if all class sizes (under (U)) or all
weights (in general) are multiplied by the same positive factor. Equal pools have equal
luck.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(1) $\Prob(\Pool<x)=1-\Prob(\Pool>x)-\Prob(\Pool=x)$, so
$\Luck(x)=2\Prob(\Pool>x)+\Prob(\Pool=x)-1=2\tilde F(x)-1$. For an independent copy
$\Pool'$, $\Luck(x)=\E[\mathbf 1\{\Pool'>x\}-\mathbf 1\{\Pool'<x\}]$, which is Cliff's
delta.

(2) $\Prob(\Pool>x)+\Prob(\Pool<x)=1-q_x<1$ and both terms are non-negative, so
$|\Luck(x)|<1$. If $x<y$ are in the support, $\Prob(\Pool>x)\ge\Prob(\Pool>y)+q_y$ and
$\Prob(\Pool<x)\le\Prob(\Pool<y)-q_x$, so $\Luck(x)>\Luck(y)$.

(3) For independent identically distributed $\Pool,\Pool'$,
$\E[\Luck(\Pool)]=\Prob(\Pool'>\Pool)-\Prob(\Pool'<\Pool)=0$ by exchangeability.

(4) Order the distinct pool sizes $x_1>\dots>x_r$ with masses $q_i$ and
$c_i=\sum_{j<i}q_j=\Prob(\Pool>x_i)$, so $\tilde F(x_i)=c_i+q_i/2$. The sum
$\sum_i[(c_i+q_i)^3-c_i^3]=1$ telescopes, so
\[
\sum_i\bigl(3c_i^2q_i+3c_iq_i^2+q_i^3\bigr)=1,\quad\text{hence}\quad
\sum_i q_ic_i^2+\sum_i c_iq_i^2=\frac{1-\sum_i q_i^3}{3}.
\]
Then $\E[\tilde F^2]=\sum_i q_i(c_i+q_i/2)^2=\sum_i q_ic_i^2+\sum_i c_iq_i^2+
\tfrac14\sum_i q_i^3=\tfrac13-\tfrac1{12}\sum_i q_i^3$. By (3), $\E[\tilde F]=\tfrac12$,
so $\Var\tilde F=\tfrac1{12}(1-\sum_i q_i^3)$ and $\Var\Luck=4\Var\tilde F$.

(5) Multiplying every weight (every size, under (U)) by $c>0$ multiplies each mass $\mu_i$
and $M$ by $c$, so each chance $q_i$ and hence each $q_x$ is unchanged.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
$\Luck$ is the mid-distribution transform of the pool \cite{lancaster1961,parzen2004},
a discrete analogue of the probability integral transform \cite{rosenblatt1952}. Giving
tied pools their mid-rank makes the mean exactly zero. If columns of a plot are as wide
as their chance, the horizontal middle is the median outcome and $\Luck$ is the
position of the landed column relative to that middle (Figure~\ref{fig:example}).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Luck under the wrong law}\label{sec:wronglaw}

Theorem~\ref{thm:luck}(3) is a statement about one law: the one that makes the class
chances $q_i$ and the one the answer is drawn from. If the answer follows another law
$\pi'$, with class chances $q'_i$, the mean of $\Luck_g$ is $\sum_iq'_i\Luck_g(s_i)$, which
is not zero in general (Section~\ref{sec:prior}: luck computed with equal chances has a
mean of $+10.6\%$ for \textsc{crane} when answers are drawn by frequency). So luck is
unbiased with respect to $\pi$ only; under (U) it is unbiased only for equal chances, and
under (F) only for frequency. The app computes luck, skill and daring with the law the
game draws by, so all three use one law.

Ties get their mid-rank. Suppose a tie rule gives tied pools weight $\lambda\in[0,1]$:
$\Luck^\lambda(x)=\Prob(\Pool>x)-\Prob(\Pool<x)+(2\lambda-1)\Prob(\Pool=x)$, so
$\lambda=\tfrac12$ is the mid-rank, $\lambda=0$ counts ties as smaller (strict
ranks) and $\lambda=1$ as larger. With $q_i$ the chances of the distinct pool sizes,
\[
\E[\Luck^\lambda(\Pool)]=(2\lambda-1)\sum_i q_i^2,
\]
because $\E[\Prob(\Pool'=\Pool)]=\sum_iq_i^2$ for an independent copy $\Pool'$ and the
$\lambda=\tfrac12$ term is zero by Theorem~\ref{thm:luck}(3). Since $\sum_iq_i^2>0$, in this
family of tie rules only the mid-rank has mean zero, and it does for every law and
every guess.

\subsection{Luck is uncorrelated with skill}

Let $\mathcal F_{t-1}$ be the history (guesses and feedback) before guess $t$. The guess
$g_t$ is a function of $\mathcal F_{t-1}$ and of a randomisation $\xi_t$ independent of
the answer and of everything before it. Let $\mathcal G_t=\sigma(\mathcal F_{t-1},\xi_t)$,
so that $g_t$ is $\mathcal G_t$-measurable and $\mathcal F_{t-1}\subseteq\mathcal G_t$.
Let $S_t$ and $\Luck_t=\Luck_{g_t}(\Pool_{g_t})$ be its skill and luck, with the
convention that $\Luck_t=0$ and $S_t=0$ once the game has ended. After the answer was
found there is one word left and one class, so Theorem~\ref{thm:luck} gives luck $0$ in
any case; after the last permitted try there is no guess and the convention applies.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:martingale}
Let the answer follow the law $\pi$ used to score. Then
$\E[\Luck_t\mid\mathcal G_t]=0$, hence $\E[\Luck_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]=0$ and
$\E\Luck_t=0$. Moreover $\Cov(\Luck_t,S_s)=0$ for $s\le t$,
$\Cov(\Luck_s,\Luck_t)=0$ for $s\ne t$, and $\sum_{s\le t}\Luck_s$ is a martingale for the
filtration $(\mathcal F_t)$. The last-try luck of Proposition~\ref{prop:last} has the
same conditional mean zero.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Given $(\mathcal F_{t-1},\xi_t)$, $\xi_t$ being independent of $W$, the answer has the law
$\pi$ restricted to the consistent words $\Omega_t$, and $g_t$ is fixed; the classes and
chances of $g_t$ are those used in the score. Theorem~\ref{thm:luck}(3) applies, so
$\E[\Luck_t\mid\mathcal G_t]=0$. The tower property gives
$\E[\Luck_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]=0$. For $s\le t$, $S_s$ is $\mathcal G_t$-measurable
(it is a function of $\Omega_s$ and $g_s$, and for $s<t$ both are in $\mathcal F_{t-1}$),
and $\Luck_s$ for $s<t$ is a function of $g_s$ and the feedback, hence
$\mathcal F_{t-1}$-measurable. All are bounded. So
$\Cov(\Luck_t,S_s)=\E[S_s\E[\Luck_t\mid\mathcal G_t]]-\E S_s\,\E\Luck_t=0$, and likewise
for $\Luck_s\Luck_t$. The partial sums are adapted to $(\mathcal F_t)$ and have
increments of conditional mean zero. On the last try the conditional mean of the win
indicator given $\mathcal G_t$ is the win chance (Proposition~\ref{prop:last}), so the
same argument applies.
\end{proof}

This is (R3). It does \emph{not} say that luck is independent of skill (the spread of
$\Luck_t$ depends on the split chosen), and it does not say that the game's total luck is
uncorrelated with its total skill: $\Cov(\Luck_t,S_s)$ for $s>t$ need not vanish, since a
lucky early outcome changes the position and so what later skills measure. The regression
of Section~\ref{sec:tries} uses game means, to which only the case $s\le t$ applies.

\subsection{Alternative luck definitions}\label{sec:alternatives}

Two definitions scale the difference between the landed pool $a$ and the expected pool
$E$. Pools are class sizes under (U) here, which is the law of the comparison in
Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:alternatives}
Let $E>1$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The expectation-scaled luck $\Luck^{\exp}(a)=(E-a)/(E-1)$ has mean zero and no
lower bound: for one pair and $N-2$ singletons ($N\ge3$), landing in the pair gives
$-(N-2)/2$.
\item The rescaled luck $\Luck^{\mathrm{res}}(a)=(E-a)/(E-1)$ for $a\le E$ and
$(E-a)/(a_{\max}-E)$ for $a>E$ lies in $[-1,1]$. If the pools are not all equal, its
mean is $\kappa\bigl(\tfrac1{E-1}-\tfrac1{a_{\max}-E}\bigr)$, $\kappa=\E[(E-\Pool)^+]>0$,
which is nonzero unless $a_{\max}-E=E-1$. For the partition $\{3,1\}$ it is $-\tfrac12$.
If all classes have equal size, $\Pool\equiv E$, $\Luck^{\mathrm{res}}\equiv0$ and the
mean is $0$ (for $\{2,2\}$, $E=2$).
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(1) $\E\Luck^{\exp}=(E-\E\Pool)/(E-1)=0$. For a pair and $N-2$ singletons
$E=(N+2)/N$, so $E-1=2/N$, $E-2=-(N-2)/N$, and the ratio is $-(N-2)/2$.
(2) If $\Pool$ is not constant then $1<E<a_{\max}$ and
$\kappa>0$. $\E[\Pool-E]=0$ gives $\E[(E-\Pool)^+]=\E[(\Pool-E)^+]=\kappa$. The positive part of
$\Luck^{\mathrm{res}}$ is scaled by $1/(E-1)$ and the negative part by $1/(a_{\max}-E)$.
For $\{3,1\}$: $N=4$, $E=\tfrac52$, $a_{\max}=3$. Landing in the $3$ (chance $\tfrac34$)
gives $-1$, landing in the $1$ gives $+1$, mean $-\tfrac12$. If $\Pool\equiv E$ every
landed pool has $a=E$ and $\Luck^{\mathrm{res}}=0$.
\end{proof}

The first violates (R2), the second (R3): its bias mixes guess quality into luck.
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} gives means from $+19\%$ to $-60\%$ on real positions.

\subsection{The last try}

On the last guess only a word that fits the clues can win, and the split is worth
nothing, so the split skill of Section~\ref{sec:skill} no longer measures the guess. Let
$N$ words fit, with law $\pi$ and total weight $M$. A guess $g\in\Omega$ wins with chance
$p(g)=\pi(g)/M$ (equal to $1/N$ under (U)); any other guess cannot win, $p(g)=0$. Let
$p^\ast=\max_{h\in\Omega}p(h)$.

\begin{definition}[Last-try skill and luck]
For $N\ge2$ the \emph{skill} of the last guess is $S^{\mathrm{last}}(g)=p(g)/p^\ast$: the
chance of winning as a share of the best chance any guess has. Its \emph{luck} is
$\Luck^{\mathrm{last}}=Y-p(g)$, with $Y=\mathbf 1\{\text{win}\}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:last}
Under the law the answer is drawn from, $\E[\Luck^{\mathrm{last}}]=0$ for every guess
(given the history and the guess). $\Luck^{\mathrm{last}}$ takes the values
$1-p$ and $-p$; for $N\ge2$ it lies in $(-1,1)$, and it is $0$ for a guess that cannot win.
$S^{\mathrm{last}}\in[0,1]$, is $1$ iff $g$ is a most likely word and $0$ iff $g$ is a
probe, and depends only on the position and the guess. Under (U), $S^{\mathrm{last}}=1$ for
every word that fits. For every guess
\[
Y=S^{\mathrm{last}}(g)\,p^\ast+\Luck^{\mathrm{last}},
\]
the part of the outcome the guess had in hand plus the part that fell by chance. A probe
has skill $0$: on the last try the loss from playing a word that cannot win is in
skill. With three words fitting under (U), a hit is $+67\%$ and a miss $-33\%$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$\E Y=p$, so $\E[Y-p]=0$. If $N\ge2$ and $p>0$ then $0<p<1$ (the weights are positive and
there is another word), so $1-p\in(0,1)$ and $-p\in(-1,0)$; if $p=0$ then $Y=0$ and the
luck is $0$. $0\le p\le p^\ast$ gives the range of $S^{\mathrm{last}}$, with equality as
stated because $p(g)=0$ iff $g\notin\Omega$ and $p(g)=p^\ast$ iff $g$ attains the maximum.
The identity is $p=(p/p^\ast)p^\ast$ added to $Y-p$. Under (U), $p=1/N=p^\ast$ for
every $g\in\Omega$.
\end{proof}

The accounting of Section~\ref{sec:accounting} is about the split and does not apply on the
last try; the identity above takes its place. The conditional mean zero is what
Theorem~\ref{thm:martingale} uses.

\section{Accounting: skill, risk and luck}\label{sec:accounting}

Skill and luck have different scales, so they do not add. Their underlying quantities
do. Let $X=\Pool_g$ be the pool landed in and $\rho=\ln(N/X)$ the \emph{total reduction}
of the candidate set (nats; divide by $\ln2$ for bits): under (U), $X$ words remain of
$N$; in general $\rho=-\ln q$ for the chance $q$ of the class landed in. With
$H=-\sum_iq_i\ln q_i$,
\[
\underbrace{H_2}_{\text{skill}}\;+\;\underbrace{H-H_2}_{\text{risk}}\;+\;
\underbrace{\rho-H}_{\text{luck}}\;=\;\rho,\qquad H_2=\ln\frac NE .
\]

\begin{proposition}[Accounting]\label{prop:accounting}
For every guess and outcome (not on the last try, Proposition~\ref{prop:last}) the three
terms sum to $\rho$, under any answer law. Moreover
\begin{enumerate}
\item the skill term $H_2=\ln(N/E)\ge0$ is the numerator of $S$;
\item the risk term $H-H_2=\ln\E[X]-\E[\ln X]\ge0$, with equality iff all classes have
equal size;
\item the luck term $Z=\rho-H=\E[\ln X]-\ln X$ has $\E[Z]=0$ (under the law the answer
follows) and $\Var Z=\Var(\ln X)$,
and under the hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{thm:martingale} it is a martingale difference.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
With $X=Nq_{i(W)}$, $\E[\rho]=\E[\ln(N/X)]=\ln N-\E\ln X=-\sum_iq_i\ln q_i=H$
and $H_2=\ln N-\ln E$. Hence
$H-H_2=\ln\E[X]-\E[\ln X]\ge0$ by Jensen's inequality, with equality iff $X$ is
constant, which by \eqref{eq:pool-law} holds iff all class sizes are equal. The luck
term is $\rho-\E\rho$: mean zero, variance $\Var\rho=\Var(\ln X)$. The conditional
statement follows as in Theorem~\ref{thm:martingale}, applied to $\ln X$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Risk]
The risk term is the convexity gap between the average of the log and the log of the
average. An even split has $H=H_2$ and zero risk. One large class and several small
ones has a large gap: low expected reduction, high payoff if the answer is in a small
class. Take $100$ words in ten classes of ten, against one class of $91$ and nine
singletons. The first has $H_2=H=3.32$ bits and risk $0$. The second has skill
$0.27$, risk $0.45$ and expected reduction $0.72$ bits. Landing in a singleton (chance
$9\%$) reduces by $6.64$ bits $=0.27+0.45+5.92$. Landing in the $91$ (chance $91\%$)
reduces by $0.14=0.27+0.45-0.59$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Two luck scores]
$Z=\E[\ln X]-\ln X$ and the percentile luck $\Luck$ are both strictly decreasing in the
landed pool $X$ and both have mean zero, so they rank outcomes identically. $Z$ has the
units of skill and risk, which makes the accounting exact, and is bounded by
$\pm\ln N$. $\Luck$ is the mid-rank of the pool and is bounded by $\pm1$.
\end{remark}

\section{Daring}\label{sec:daring}

Risk is a property of a guess. It does not say what the player was trying to do. A
guess that could be the answer is an attempt at the word; one that cannot is a probe,
chosen for what it tells. \emph{Daring} measures how much a guess was an attempt at the
whole word rather than a way of narrowing the pool. Fix a position with $\tau\ge1$ tries
left, this one included, a set $\Omega$ of $N\ge2$ words still possible and the answer law
$\pi$ with total weight $M$ (Section~\ref{sec:laws}), the same law that scores skill and
luck. Let $S(g)\in[0,1]$ be the skill of $g$ (the split skill of Section~\ref{sec:skill};
daring is $0$ when $\tau=1$, so the last-try skill is not needed), and
\[
p(g)=\frac{\pi(g)}{M}\ \text{ if } g\in\Omega,\qquad p(g)=0\ \text{ otherwise,}
\]
the chance that $g$ wins now. The reference for ``going for the word'' is a word that is
still possible, as drawn by the answer law: the indicator and $p$ compare $g$ with the
words the law could have drawn.

\begin{definition}[Daring]\label{def:daring}
The daring of a guess $g$ is
\[
D(g)=\mathbf 1[g\in\Omega]\;\bigl(1-S(g)\bigr)\;\bigl(1-p(g)\bigr)\;\Bigl(1-\frac1\tau\Bigr)\in[0,1),
\]
quoted in percent. It is undefined when only one word is possible.
\end{definition}

The four factors say, in turn: the guess is a try at the word; it gave up some of the
splitting the best guess would have done; the word was unlikely; and there were tries to
spare. With one try left going for the word is the only play, so $D=0$. A guess that is
both the best split and a possible answer costs nothing, so $D=0$ too.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:daring}
\begin{enumerate}
\item $0\le D(g)<1$, and it depends on the position, the law, the choice of $g$ and $\tau$
only, not on the answer, so (R1) holds.
\item $D(g)=0$ if $g\notin\Omega$, if $\tau=1$, or if $S(g)=1$. (The case $p(g)=1$ would
need every other possible word to have chance $0$; the weights are positive, so it means
$N=1$, where $D$ is undefined and the case does not arise.)
\item As a function of $(S,p,\tau)$, $D$ is non-increasing in $S$ and in $p$ and
non-decreasing in $\tau$, the others fixed.
\item Let $g\in\Omega$ and $\tau\ge2$. Then $p(g)\in(0,1)$ and $S(g)>0$, so
$D(g)<(1-\tfrac1\tau)(1-p(g))<1-\tfrac1\tau$: the value $1-1/\tau$ is never attained. More
precisely $S(g)\ge s_0(p)=\ln\!\bigl(1/(1-2p(1-p))\bigr)/\ln(N/E^\ast)$ with $p=p(g)$, so
$D(g)\le\bigl(1-\min\{1,s_0(p)\}\bigr)(1-p)(1-\tfrac1\tau)$.
\item At the opening guess every word is possible. If $\Omega$ is the whole list and $g$ is
in it, then $\mathbf 1[g\in\Omega]=1$ and $D(g)=(1-S(g))(1-p(g))(1-\tfrac1\tau)$, with
$\tau=6$ in the classic game, so $1-\tfrac1\tau=\tfrac56$. Under (U), $p=1/N$ is the same for every guess and $D$ is a
function of skill alone, $D=\tfrac56(1-\tfrac1N)(1-S)$. Under (F), $D$ lies between
$\tfrac56(1-p^\ast)(1-S)$ and $\tfrac56(1-S)$, $p^\ast=\max_hp(h)$.
\item $D$ does not involve risk, so the accounting of Proposition~\ref{prop:accounting}
is unaffected.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(1)--(3) are read off the product: each factor lies in $[0,1]$, the last is below $1$,
each is monotone in the stated variable, and the factors do not mention the answer.
(4) $g\in\Omega$ and the weights are positive, so $p(g)>0$, and $p(g)<1$ since
$N\ge2$. The class of $g$ is $\{g\}$ (all green iff equal), of chance $p$, and the other
classes share chance $1-p$, so $\sum_iq_i^2\le p^2+(1-p)^2=1-2p(1-p)<1$. Hence
$E(g)<N$, $S(g)>0$, and $\ln(N/E(g))=-\ln\sum_iq_i^2\ge-\ln(1-2p(1-p))$; divide by
$\ln(N/E^\ast)$. (5) $\mathbf 1[g\in\Omega]=1$ for every $g$ in the list $\Omega$; the rest is
substitution, with $0<p\le p^\ast$ and $p=1/N$ under (U).
\end{proof}

At the opening, then, daring tells no more than skill does: \textsc{crane}
($S=0.816$) has $D=15.3$ and an opener of median skill ($S=0.674$) has $D=27.2$, both
under (U) and $\tau=6$. Daring separates players by what they try only through the
guesses after the opener.

\begin{remark}
Daring is reported beside the accounting and is not a term of it. An earlier definition
counted, among the guesses of higher skill, the share that were less risky; it
mostly measured how far a guess was from the best one, so any player who was not
optimal looked daring, and it ignored the tries left. It is replaced because the
question a player means by ``bold'' is whether they went for the word. Skill is relative
to the language and the word length, so $D$ is too, through $S$. The chance $p$ uses the
same law as skill and luck. Daring is defined for single-board games: the quartet and the
relay race of Section~\ref{sec:multi} have no daring score.
\end{remark}

\section{Several boards}\label{sec:multi}

Two variants hide several words. In a \emph{quartet} one guess is played on every
unsolved board at once. In a \emph{relay race} boards are played in turn, and every
earlier guess is applied to a board when it opens.

A guess is made for live boards $b=1,\dots,B$ with possible sets $\Omega_b$ of sizes
$N_b$. Skill $S_b$ and luck $\Luck_b$ are computed per board as above, with $E_b^\ast$
the best real guess for that board and every board scored by the same law $\pi$ (the
weights are threaded through to each board, restricted to its candidates). Let $w_b=\log_2\max\{N_b,2\}$, which depends only on
the history, and
\[
S=\frac{\sum_b w_bS_b}{\sum_b w_b},\qquad
\Luck=\frac{\sum_b w_b\Luck_b}{\sum_b w_b}.
\]

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:multi}
$S\in[0,1]$ and $-1<\Luck<1$. Suppose that, given the history and the guess, the
answer of each live board has, as a marginal, the law $\pi$ restricted to that board's
candidates, which is the law the board is scored by. Then
$\E[\Luck\mid\mathcal G_t]=0$ and Theorem~\ref{thm:martingale} holds for the combined
luck. Independence between boards is not required.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Convex combinations stay in $[0,1]$ and $(-1,1)$. The weights are
$\mathcal F_{t-1}$-measurable, so
$\E[\Luck\mid\mathcal G_t]=\sum_b w_b\E[\Luck_b\mid\mathcal G_t]/\sum_bw_b=0$
by Theorem~\ref{thm:luck}(3) on each board, which uses only the marginal law of that
board's answer. The remainder of Theorem~\ref{thm:martingale}'s proof uses only this.
\end{proof}

The hypothesis is about how the game draws the boards' answers. It holds if each board's
answer is an independent draw from $\pi$ (under (U) or (F)). It does not hold exactly if
the answers are drawn jointly, for instance all distinct or preferring sets that share
few letters: then a board's marginal law given the history is not $\pi$ restricted to its
candidates, and the combined luck need not have mean zero. How far the quartet's draw
departs from the hypothesis is not measured here, so the zero-mean property is proved
for independent draws only. (The earlier statement, with equal chances on every board,
is the case (U) with independent draws.)

\begin{remark}[Weights]
$\log_2N_b$ is the number of bits still to find on board $b$. A board with one word left
has weight one: playing that word is rewarded and missing it is penalised; its skill is
$1$ if the guess is that word and $0$ otherwise. Uniform weights would let a nearly
solved board dominate. The weights are a design choice.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Head start]
In a relay race a guess on the active board narrows boards not yet open. This credit is
not scored. It is reported: the \emph{head start} of a board is the number of words
possible on it when it opens, with all earlier guesses applied, against the size of the
list. An earlier guess can solve a board as it opens. Assigning this credit to the
earlier guesses is open.
\end{remark}

\section{Computation}\label{sec:computation}

Feedback for an $\ell$-letter guess is packed into an integer in $\{0,\dots,3^\ell-1\}$. For a
guess $g$, class sizes (or class masses, under a weighted law) are counted in an array
indexed by pattern, reset by revisiting
only the patterns touched. $E(g)=N\sum q_i^2$ costs one pass, $O(\ell N)$, with no
allocation. Skill
needs $E^\ast=\min_gE(g)$, a scan of all $|\mathcal G|$ guesses: $O(\ell N|\mathcal G|)$,
about $1.2\times10^8$ operations for a first guess on $N=|\mathcal G|=4{,}994$
five-letter words, and fewer as $N$ falls. The scan also gives the skill of every
guess, so the rank of the player's guess is free. For several boards, boards sharing
the same candidate list (all of them at the start) are scored once. A truncated scan
gives $E^\ast$ too large and skill too high; the app scans every guess.

Luck needs the class masses of the player's guess only: sort the distinct masses and
accumulate the chances above and below, $O(k\log k)$ for $k$ classes. The last-try skill
needs the largest weight among the words that fit.

\subsection{Do skill, luck and daring predict the tries?}\label{sec:tries}

If skill, luck and daring describe a game, they should predict how many tries it takes.
Two tools test this. The first, \texttt{app/tools/predict\_tries.dart}, plays $400$ games
per language by simulated
players whose habits are drawn afresh for each game (the chance, per guess, of each of
best, best-fit, frequent, random-fit and wild play), and fits the number of tries (a
failed game counts as $7$) by least squares on the game's mean skill, mean luck and mean
daring over the guesses with a choice. Table~\ref{tab:tries} gives $R^2$. It should not be
read as evidence, for three reasons. Answers were drawn by frequency while luck assumed
equal chances, so the luck column is biased and not uncorrelated with skill
(Section~\ref{sec:wronglaw}). The model is fitted and read on the same games, from one
seed: the same model gave $R^2=0.542$ at $250$ games and $0.483$ at $400$, so differences
of a few points are within noise. And a lost game coded $7$ is a censored value treated
as exact, game means over guesses with a choice are endogenous (a short game averages few
guesses), and the last-try luck is the win indicator less its chance, so it nearly encodes
the outcome of a game that reaches its last try.

The second, \texttt{app/tools/predict\_tries3.dart}, replaces it. It scores every guess
with the app's own code under an explicit answer law: \textbf{u} (equal chances, frequency
off), \textbf{f} (by frequency) and \textbf{d} (the daily: equal chances over the $3{,}000$
most common words). The same mixed-habit players play games whose answers are drawn by
that law, and the tries are regressed on the means of skill, luck and daring. The fit uses
the first half of the games and $R^2$ is read on the second half, which the fit never saw.
The tool also prints how evenly each score fills its range (the share of all guesses in
each tenth), the correlations between the three game means and their variance inflation
factors, which show whether the coefficients can be read one at a time. It uses one seed. As in the first experiment, the last try's luck (outcome less
win chance) enters the game means, so part of the luck $R^2$ is mechanical.

\subsection{A worked game}\label{sec:game}

\texttt{worked\_game.py} follows one game: \textsc{caret}, then \textsc{least}, against
\textsc{agent}. After \textsc{caret} $18$ words remain. \textsc{least} splits them into
classes of $5,3,2,2,1,1,1,1,1,1$. Its expected pool is $E=2.67$. The best real guess is
\textsc{sepal}, with $E^\ast=1.56$ and classes $3,2,2$ and eleven singletons. \textsc{least}
scores $78$; \textsc{sepal} scores $100$.

The answer falls in the class of $5$ under \textsc{least}: reduction $1.85$ bits
$=2.75$ (skill) $+\,0.28$ (risk) $-\,1.19$ (luck), percentile luck $-72\%$. The best guess
\textsc{sepal} has skill $3.53$ and risk $0.15$ bits. \textsc{least} is a possible answer,
so it finishes the game at once with chance $1/18=5.6\%$; \textsc{sepal} is not a possible
answer and cannot. This is a gamble taken to finish in fewer guesses. It is priced in the
risk term ($0.28$ against $0.15$ bits), but only as a singleton class. The accounting
gives a win no more credit than any other singleton, although a win saves a whole guess
(Section~\ref{sec:open}).

\subsection{The game's answer draw}\label{sec:prior}

Luck computed with equal chances has mean zero when each possible word is equally likely.
The game draws answers by word frequency. \texttt{prior\_bias.py} gives the mean
first-guess luck, computed with equal chances, when the answer is drawn by frequency:
$+10.2\%$ for \textsc{tares}, $+10.6\%$ for \textsc{crane}, $+7.6\%$ for \textsc{slate},
$-6.4\%$ for \textsc{abbey}, $-8.2\%$ for \textsc{mamma}. Restricting the draw to the $500$
most common words changes these by about one point. Common words tend to fall in small
classes of strong guesses and large classes of weak ones. Under (U) the means are zero to
machine precision (Table~\ref{tab:luck}). This cross-law bias is what
Section~\ref{sec:wronglaw} describes; the app now computes luck with the law the answer is
drawn by, for which the mean is exactly zero (Theorem~\ref{thm:luck}(3)). The opposite
direction (luck by frequency when answers are equal) was not measured.

\subsection{A bound on the best conceivable split}

Three bounds on the number of groups were tested: the naive $\min(N,3^\ell)$; a bound $B$
counting $2$ outcomes at positions fixed by the clues and $3$ elsewhere; and a bound $C$
taking, over all real guesses, the product over letters of how many of
green/yellow/grey that letter could show against the words still possible. On $14$
simulated games ($40$ positions after $0$, $1$ or $2$ guesses), $B$ and $C$ equal the naive
bound in every position. Where $N>243$ the best scanned real guess made about $63\%$ of the
allowed groups. Where $N\le243$ a real guess separated every word in $3$ of $20$
positions. $C$ is not a valid bound for repeated letters ($7$ of $109{,}782$ guesses
exceeded it, all with a repeated letter). The bounds do not give a reachable best, so skill
is normalised by the best real guess.

\subsection{Numerical check of the theorems}

\texttt{verify\_theorems.py} checks Theorems~\ref{thm:luck} and~\ref{thm:martingale} and
Proposition~\ref{prop:alternatives} independently of the app, under (U). On $20{,}000$ random
partitions, luck lies strictly in $(-1,1)$, is strictly monotone in the pool, is unchanged
by scaling the classes, has $|\text{mean}|<2\times10^{-16}$, and has variance equal to
$(1-\sum q^3)/3$ to the same precision. On $1{,}500$ simulated games of four guesses, with
uniform answers and guesses depending only on the past, the mean luck of each guess is
within sampling error of zero (for the first guess $+0.008$, standard deviation $0.57$).
All correlations of a guess's luck with the skill of any guess so far, and with earlier
luck, are within four standard errors of zero (largest in magnitude $0.084$, tolerance
$0.103$); the ``skill'' there is the linear $(N-E)/(N-1)$, a number fixed by the past,
which is all the theorem needs.

\texttt{verify\_laws.py} checks the weighted-law statements. With rational arithmetic, on
$3{,}000$ random weighted partitions (ties included) luck has mean exactly $0$, variance
$(1-\sum q^3)/3$, lies strictly in $(-1,1)$ and is strictly monotone; luck under a
different law has a nonzero mean; the tie rule with weight $\lambda$ has mean
$(2\lambda-1)\sum q^2$; the rescaled luck has mean $0$ for $\{2,2\}$ and $-\tfrac12$ for
$\{3,1\}$; the last-try luck has mean $0$ and the daring bound of
Proposition~\ref{prop:daring}(4) holds. On $800$ simulated games of three guesses by a
randomising player, with answers drawn by frequency from the $250$ most common words and
luck computed with that law, the mean luck of each guess is within four standard errors
of zero and all correlations with the skill so far and with earlier luck are within
tolerance (\texttt{results/verify\_laws.txt}; the skill there uses a sample of $50$
guesses for $E^\ast$, again a number fixed by the past).

\section{Open problems}\label{sec:open}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Non-uniform priors.} \emph{Answered} (Section~\ref{sec:laws}): replace sizes
by effective sizes $s_i=Nq_i$, so $E=N\sum_iq_i^2$; Theorems~\ref{thm:luck}
and~\ref{thm:martingale} hold unchanged, pools are ranked by mass, and luck is exactly
unbiased under the law used (and biased under another, Section~\ref{sec:wronglaw}).
What remains open is whether a player can be assumed to know the frequencies.
\item \textbf{Lookahead.} Skill scores the split made now. A guess can score lower and
play better over the rest of the game. Is there a skill that credits lookahead and
depends only on the player's knowledge, for example through the expected number of
further guesses of an optimal continuation \cite{bertsimas2022}?
\item \textbf{Scale of skill.} The logarithm of the expected pool is used because the
linear scale compresses first guesses near $100$. Other R\'enyi orders, or a scale
calibrated to the guesses a strong player needs, are alternatives. Test against how strong
players rank openings.
\item \textbf{Risk attitude and tries left.} The best entropy order should depend on the
guesses remaining: an even split with many left, a gamble with few (Section~\ref{sec:risk}).
Is there a skill that depends on the tries left, for example the chance of finishing within
them under an optimal continuation?
\item \textbf{Credit for finishing now.} A guess that is a possible answer wins at
once with chance $p(g)$ ($1/N$ under (U)); on the last try this is now the skill
(Proposition~\ref{prop:last}), but not before it. In the accounting the winning class is a singleton like any
other, with reduction $\ln N$. But landing in another singleton still costs one more
guess; landing in the winning class does not. Skill and risk therefore undercount
guesses that can win. A measure based on the expected number of remaining guesses would
count it. How should this be combined with the entropy terms?
\item \textbf{Total luck of a game.} Is there a normalised total luck comparable across
games of different length, for example $\sum_t\Luck_t/\sqrt{\sum_t\Var\Luck_t}$?
\item \textbf{Relay-race credit.} How to credit the head start of a board to the earlier
guesses that produced it.
\item \textbf{Ties.} \emph{Answered} (Section~\ref{sec:wronglaw}): a rule giving tied pools
weight $\lambda$ has mean $(2\lambda-1)\sum_iq_i^2$, so within that family the mid-rank
$\lambda=\tfrac12$ is the only one that is fair for every law. Rules outside the family
are not covered.
\item \textbf{Hard modes, other feedback.} Only the following is used about $f$: a map to
a finite set with all-green exactly at equality. \emph{Answered, largely trivially}: in a game where later guesses must obey earlier
clues, every word still possible obeys them (the answer does), so $\Omega\subseteq\mathcal G$
still holds and all results go through with $\mathcal G$ replaced by the legal guesses;
only $E^\ast$ can rise, because the probes are no longer available. How much it rises is
not measured.
\item \textbf{Playstyles on real players.} The thresholds of Section~\ref{sec:styles}
come from simulated players at five letters. They should be set on real play, and
probably per length: skill is not comparable across lengths (the player who ignores the
clues scores $48$ at five letters and $69$ at eight). Is there a normalisation of
skill and daring, for example by their distribution over a reference set of players, that
makes one threshold fit all?
\item \textbf{The form of daring.} $D$ is a product of four factors chosen to be
monotone in what a bold choice means. Other forms (a sum, other powers, the chance in bits
rather than as a probability) would fit the tries better or worse. Which form predicts
the tries best, and does a fitted form beat the product?
\item \textbf{Approximating $E^\ast$.} The exact scan is quadratic in the list size.
Are there provable shortcuts, such as a lower bound on $E^\ast$ from letter statistics of
$\Omega$, for very large lists or scripts with more patterns?
\end{enumerate}
