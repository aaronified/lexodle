\begin{abstract}
We define skill, risk and luck for a guess in a word game with colour feedback
(Wordle and variants). A guess partitions the words still possible by the feedback
they would give. The answer lies in one class; its size is the \emph{pool}.
\emph{Skill} is the log-reduction of the expected pool, as a fraction of the same
quantity for the best real guess. \emph{Luck} is the probability of a larger pool
minus the probability of a smaller one. It lies strictly in $(-100,100)$ percent,
has mean zero for every guess, and its increments over a game are martingale
differences, so luck is uncorrelated with skill. The two simpler luck definitions
are unbounded or biased. The log-reduction of the candidate set equals skill plus
risk plus luck exactly, where risk is the gap between Shannon and collision
entropy. We extend the definitions to games with several boards, give an exact
algorithm, and report experiments on an English five-letter list ($N=4{,}994$).
The best first guess leaves $114.4$ words on average. \textsc{crane} scores
$81.6$ ($97.6$ on a linear scale, which puts most openings near $100$). A bound on
the best conceivable split from the number of feedback patterns is too loose to
use. We list open problems.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

In Wordle-like games the player guesses a word. Each letter is marked as in the
right place, elsewhere in the word, or absent. After the game two questions arise.
Skill: given what was known, how good was the guess? Luck: given the guess, how
favourable was the feedback? A score that mixes the two is uninformative.

Entropy-based solvers choose the guess that maximises the expected information
in the feedback \cite{shannon1948,sanderson2022}, as for the code game
Mastermind \cite{knuth1977,kooi2005}. Exact methods give optimal strategies for the
original word list \cite{bertsimas2022}. For generalised word lists, deciding
whether a win within a given number of guesses can be guaranteed is NP-hard
\cite{lokshtanov2022}. A newspaper review tool reports skill and luck per guess
\cite{nyt2022}; we know of no published formula. This paper does not address how to
play. It defines scores to show a player after the game. The requirements are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item[(R1)] \textbf{Knowledge only.} Neither score depends on the answer except
through what the player saw.
\item[(R2)] \textbf{Bounded.} Skill runs from $0$ (the guess tells nothing) to $100$
(as good as any real guess). Luck is symmetric about $0$ and bounded.
\item[(R3)] \textbf{Fair luck.} Luck has mean zero and is uncorrelated with skill.
\item[(R4)] \textbf{One plot.} Both scores can be read from a single plot of the
outcomes the guess could have had.
\item[(R5)] \textbf{Accounting.} The reduction of the candidate set equals a skill
term plus a risk term plus a luck term, exactly (Section~\ref{sec:accounting}).
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:setting} fixes the setting, Section~\ref{sec:skill} defines skill,
Section~\ref{sec:luck} luck, Section~\ref{sec:accounting} the accounting,
Section~\ref{sec:multi} several boards, Section~\ref{sec:computation} the algorithm,
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} the experiments, Section~\ref{sec:open} the open
problems. Code and scripts are listed in Section~\ref{sec:repro}.

\section{Setting}\label{sec:setting}

Let $\Omega$ be the finite set of words still possible given everything the player
has seen, $N=|\Omega|\ge1$, and $\mathcal G$ the finite set of admissible guesses.
Assume $\Omega\subseteq\mathcal G$. Let $\Phi$ be the set of feedback patterns
($\{\text{grey},\text{yellow},\text{green}\}^n$ for $n$-letter words) and
$f\colon\mathcal G\times\Omega\to\Phi$ the feedback function. Only two properties of
$f$ are used: it is computable, and $f(g,w)$ is all green only if $g=w$.

\begin{definition}[Prior (U)]
The answer $A$ is uniform on $\Omega$. Given the history of guesses and feedback, $A$
is uniform on the words consistent with it.
\end{definition}

(U) is the player's view: without knowing how the game draws answers, all possible
words count equally. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} measures the effect of the real
draw.

A guess $g\in\mathcal G$ partitions $\Omega$ into nonempty classes
$C_\varphi=\{w\in\Omega: f(g,w)=\varphi\}$ of sizes $s_1,\dots,s_k$,
$\sum_is_i=N$. The answer lands in one class. Its size $\Pool_g=|C_{f(g,A)}|$ is the
\emph{pool}, the number of words still possible after the guess. If $m_x$ classes
have size $x$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pool-law}
\Prob(\Pool_g=x)=\frac{x\,m_x}{N}.
\end{equation}
Pools are size-biased: a large class is more likely to contain the answer.

\section{Skill}\label{sec:skill}

\begin{definition}[Expected pool]
$E(g)=\E[\Pool_g]=\dfrac1N\sum_{i=1}^k s_i^2$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Best real guess, skill]
$E^\ast=\min_{g\in\mathcal G}E(g)$. For $N\ge2$ the \emph{skill} of $g\in\mathcal G$ is
\[
S(g)=\frac{\ln\bigl(N/E(g)\bigr)}{\ln\bigl(N/E^\ast\bigr)}\in[0,1],
\]
reported as a percentage. For $N=1$, $S(g)=1$ if $g$ is the remaining word and $0$
otherwise.
\end{definition}

The logarithm is used because a guess cuts the candidate set by a factor. On the linear
scale $(N-E)/(N-E^\ast)$ most sensible guesses score near $100$
(Section~\ref{sec:experiments}). The linear score has the same endpoints and the same
ordering of guesses.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:E}
Let the partition of $g$ have $k$ classes.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\max\{1,N/k\}\le E(g)\le N$; $E(g)=N$ iff $k=1$; $E(g)=1$ iff $k=N$;
$E(g)=N/k$ iff all classes have equal size.
\item If the partition of $g'$ properly refines that of $g$, then $E(g')<E(g)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
(1) $1\le s_i\le N$ gives $E\ge1$, with equality iff every $s_i=1$, and $s_i^2\le Ns_i$
gives $E\le N$, with equality iff $k=1$. By Cauchy--Schwarz
$\sum s_i^2\ge(\sum s_i)^2/k=N^2/k$, with equality iff the $s_i$ are equal.
(2) Splitting a class of size $a_1+\dots+a_r$, $r\ge2$, replaces $(\sum a_j)^2$ by
$\sum a_j^2$, which is smaller because the cross terms are positive.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:skill}
Let $N\ge2$ and $\Omega\subseteq\mathcal G$. Then $E^\ast<N$, so $S$ is defined, and
\begin{enumerate}
\item $0\le S(g)\le1$ for every $g\in\mathcal G$; $S(g)=1$ iff $E(g)=E^\ast$;
$S(g)=0$ iff $g$ does not divide $\Omega$;
\item $S$ is strictly decreasing in $E(g)$ and affine in $\ln E(g)$: halving the
expected pool adds $\ln2/\ln(N/E^\ast)$. If the partition of $g'$ properly refines
that of $g$, then $S(g')>S(g)$;
\item $S(g)$ is a function of $(\Omega,g,\mathcal G)$ alone. It does not depend on the
answer.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Take $w\in\Omega\subseteq\mathcal G$. Only $w$ gives all green against $w$, so
$\{w\}$ is a class of $w$'s partition. For $N\ge2$ the partition is nontrivial, so
$E(w)<N$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:E} and $E^\ast<N$. The denominator is positive.
(1) follows from $E^\ast\le E(g)\le N$ and the lemma, (2) from the formula and
Lemma~\ref{lem:E}(2), (3) because $E(g)$ and $E^\ast$ are computed from the classes of
$\Omega$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Entropy]
Let $p_i=s_i/N$. Then $\ln(N/E)=-\ln\sum p_i^2=H_2$, the R\'enyi entropy of order $2$
\cite{renyi1961}; $e^{H_2}$ is the inverse Simpson index, the Hill number of order $2$
\cite{simpson1949,hill1973}. Hence $S(g)=H_2(g)/\max_{g'}H_2(g')$. The Shannon
entropy $H=-\sum p_i\ln p_i=\ln N-\E[\ln\Pool_g]$ \cite{shannon1948} is the expected
log-reduction of the candidate set, and $H\ge H_2$ by Jensen. $H_2$ is used because it
is a function of $E$, the expected number of words left. Other orders of R\'enyi entropy
give other scales (Section~\ref{sec:risk}).
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Why the best real guess]
A bound that needs no search is $e=N/\min(N,3^n)$, the most even split into
$\min(N,3^n)$ groups. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} tests it and a sharper bound from
the clues. Both are too loose. The best real word makes about two thirds of the groups
the bound allows when many words remain, and rarely separates every word when few
remain. $E^\ast$ is therefore the actual minimum over real guesses.
\end{remark}

\section{Luck}\label{sec:luck}

Let $q_x=\Prob(\Pool_g=x)=x\,m_x/N$.

\begin{definition}[Luck]
For $x$ in the support of $\Pool=\Pool_g$,
\[
\Luck_g(x)=\Prob(\Pool>x)-\Prob(\Pool<x).
\]
The luck of an outcome is $\Luck_g(\Pool_g)$, reported as a percentage.
\end{definition}

It is the probability of a larger pool minus the probability of a smaller one: negative
if the outcome is worse than most, positive if better, zero at the median.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:luck}
Let $\Pool$ have the law \eqref{eq:pool-law} and $\Luck=\Luck_g$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item (Mid-distribution form.) $\Luck(x)=2\tilde F(x)-1$ with
$\tilde F(x)=\Prob(\Pool>x)+\tfrac12\Prob(\Pool=x)$. $\Luck(\Pool)$ is Cliff's delta
between the realised pool and an independent copy \cite{cliff1993}.
\item (Bounded.) $-1<\Luck(x)<1$ for every $x$ in the support, and $\Luck$ is strictly
decreasing in $x$.
\item (Fair.) $\E[\Luck(\Pool)]=0$ for every guess.
\item (Spread.) $\Var\,\Luck(\Pool)=\dfrac{1-\sum_x q_x^3}{3}<\dfrac13$.
\item (Invariance.) $\Luck(x)$ is unchanged if all class sizes are multiplied by the
same positive factor. Equal pools have equal luck.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(1) $\Prob(\Pool<x)=1-\Prob(\Pool>x)-\Prob(\Pool=x)$, so
$\Luck(x)=2\Prob(\Pool>x)+\Prob(\Pool=x)-1=2\tilde F(x)-1$. For an independent copy
$\Pool'$, $\Luck(x)=\E[\mathbf 1\{\Pool'>x\}-\mathbf 1\{\Pool'<x\}]$, which is Cliff's
delta.

(2) $\Prob(\Pool>x)+\Prob(\Pool<x)=1-q_x<1$ and both terms are non-negative, so
$|\Luck(x)|<1$. If $x<y$ are in the support, $\Prob(\Pool>x)\ge\Prob(\Pool>y)+q_y$ and
$\Prob(\Pool<x)\le\Prob(\Pool<y)-q_x$, so $\Luck(x)>\Luck(y)$.

(3) For independent identically distributed $\Pool,\Pool'$,
$\E[\Luck(\Pool)]=\Prob(\Pool'>\Pool)-\Prob(\Pool'<\Pool)=0$ by exchangeability.

(4) Order the distinct pool sizes $x_1>\dots>x_r$ with masses $q_i$ and
$c_i=\sum_{j<i}q_j=\Prob(\Pool>x_i)$, so $\tilde F(x_i)=c_i+q_i/2$. The sum
$\sum_i[(c_i+q_i)^3-c_i^3]=1$ telescopes, so
\[
\sum_i\bigl(3c_i^2q_i+3c_iq_i^2+q_i^3\bigr)=1,\quad\text{hence}\quad
\sum_i q_ic_i^2+\sum_i c_iq_i^2=\frac{1-\sum_i q_i^3}{3}.
\]
Then $\E[\tilde F^2]=\sum_i q_i(c_i+q_i/2)^2=\sum_i q_ic_i^2+\sum_i c_iq_i^2+
\tfrac14\sum_i q_i^3=\tfrac13-\tfrac1{12}\sum_i q_i^3$. By (3), $\E[\tilde F]=\tfrac12$,
so $\Var\tilde F=\tfrac1{12}(1-\sum_i q_i^3)$ and $\Var\Luck=4\Var\tilde F$.

(5) Multiplying every $s_i$ by $c>0$ multiplies $N$ and each numerator $x\,m_x$ by $c$,
so each $q_x$ is unchanged.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
$\Luck$ is the mid-distribution transform of the pool \cite{lancaster1961,parzen2004},
a discrete analogue of the probability integral transform \cite{rosenblatt1952}. Giving
tied pools their mid-rank makes the mean exactly zero. If columns of a plot are as wide
as their chance, the horizontal middle is the median outcome and $\Luck$ is the
position of the landed column relative to that middle (Figure~\ref{fig:example}).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Luck is uncorrelated with skill}

Let $\mathcal F_{t-1}$ be the history before guess $t$. The guess $g_t$ is a function
of $\mathcal F_{t-1}$ and possibly independent randomness. Let $S_t$ and
$\Luck_t=\Luck_{g_t}(\Pool_{g_t})$ be its skill and luck.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:martingale}
Under (U), $\E[\Luck_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]=0$. Hence $\E\Luck_t=0$,
$\Cov(\Luck_t,S_s)=0$ for $s\le t$, $\Cov(\Luck_s,\Luck_t)=0$ for $s\ne t$, and
$\sum_{s\le t}\Luck_s$ is a martingale.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Given $\mathcal F_{t-1}$ the answer is uniform on the consistent words $\Omega_t$ and
$g_t$ is fixed, so Theorem~\ref{thm:luck}(3) applies conditionally. $S_s$ ($s\le t$) and
$\Luck_s$ ($s<t$) are $\mathcal F_{t-1}$-measurable, so
$\Cov(\Luck_t,S_s)=\E[S_s\E[\Luck_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]]-\E S_s\,\E\Luck_t=0$, and
likewise for $\Luck_s\Luck_t$.
\end{proof}

This is (R3).

\subsection{Alternative luck definitions}\label{sec:alternatives}

Two definitions scale the difference between the landed pool $a$ and the expected pool
$E$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:alternatives}
Let $E>1$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The expectation-scaled luck $\Luck^{\exp}(a)=(E-a)/(E-1)$ has mean zero and no
lower bound: for one pair and $N-2$ singletons, landing in the pair gives $-(N-2)/2$.
\item The rescaled luck $\Luck^{\mathrm{res}}(a)=(E-a)/(E-1)$ for $a\le E$ and
$(E-a)/(a_{\max}-E)$ for $a>E$ lies in $[-1,1]$. Its mean is
$\kappa\bigl(\tfrac1{E-1}-\tfrac1{a_{\max}-E}\bigr)$, $\kappa=\E[(E-\Pool)^+]$, which is
nonzero unless $a_{\max}-E=E-1$. For the partition $\{3,1\}$ it is $-\tfrac12$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(1) $\E\Luck^{\exp}=(E-\E\Pool)/(E-1)=0$. For a pair and $N-2$ singletons
$E=(N+2)/N$, so $E-1=2/N$, $E-2=-(N-2)/N$, and the ratio is $-(N-2)/2$.
(2) $\E[\Pool-E]=0$ gives $\E[(E-\Pool)^+]=\E[(\Pool-E)^+]=\kappa$. The positive part of
$\Luck^{\mathrm{res}}$ is scaled by $1/(E-1)$ and the negative part by $1/(a_{\max}-E)$.
For $\{3,1\}$: $N=4$, $E=\tfrac52$, $a_{\max}=3$. Landing in the $3$ (chance $\tfrac34$)
gives $-1$, landing in the $1$ gives $+1$, mean $-\tfrac12$.
\end{proof}

The first violates (R2), the second (R3): its bias mixes guess quality into luck.
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} gives means from $+19\%$ to $-60\%$ on real positions.

\subsection{The last try}

On the last guess only a word that fits the clues can win. Let $n$ words fit. A guess
that is one of them wins with chance $1/n$. Any other guess cannot win.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:last}
Let $Y=\mathbf 1\{\text{win}\}$ and $\pi=\Prob(\text{win})\in\{1/n,0\}$. The last-try
luck $\Luck=Y-\pi$ has mean zero for every guess, takes the values $1-\pi$ and $-\pi$,
and lies in $(-1,1)$ for $n\ge2$. It is $0$ for a guess that cannot win; that loss
belongs to skill. With three words fitting, a hit is $+67\%$ and a miss $-33\%$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$\E Y=\pi$, so $\E[Y-\pi]=0$. The range follows from $0\le\pi\le\tfrac12$ for $n\ge2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:twenty}
Twenty words are split into groups of $10,5,3,1,1$. Then $E=(100+25+9+1+1)/20=6.8$. If
no real guess beats singletons ($E^\ast=1$), the skill is $\ln(20/6.8)/\ln20=36.0\%$
($(20-6.8)/19=69.5\%$ on the linear scale). Luck by landing: in the $10$,
$0-\tfrac{10}{20}=-50\%$; in the $5$, $\tfrac{10}{20}-\tfrac{5}{20}=+25\%$; in the $3$,
$\tfrac{15}{20}-\tfrac2{20}=+65\%$; in a $1$, $\tfrac{18}{20}-0=+90\%$ (the two
singletons tie and neither counts against the other). The chance-weighted mean is
$\tfrac{10}{20}(-.5)+\tfrac5{20}(.25)+\tfrac3{20}(.65)+\tfrac2{20}(.9)=0$.
\end{example}

\section{Accounting: skill, risk and luck}\label{sec:accounting}

Skill and luck have different scales, so they do not add. Their underlying quantities
do. Let $\rho=\ln(N/A)$ be the \emph{total reduction} of the candidate set (nats; divide
by $\ln2$ for bits): $A$ words remain of $N$. With $X=\Pool_g$ and
$H=-\sum_ip_i\ln p_i$,
\[
\underbrace{H_2}_{\text{skill}}\;+\;\underbrace{H-H_2}_{\text{risk}}\;+\;
\underbrace{\rho-H}_{\text{luck}}\;=\;\rho,\qquad H_2=\ln\frac NE .
\]

\begin{proposition}[Accounting]\label{prop:accounting}
For every guess and outcome the three terms sum to $\rho$. Moreover
\begin{enumerate}
\item the skill term $H_2=\ln(N/E)\ge0$ is the numerator of $S$;
\item the risk term $H-H_2=\ln\E[X]-\E[\ln X]\ge0$, with equality iff all classes have
equal size;
\item the luck term $Z=\rho-H=\E[\ln X]-\ln A$ has $\E[Z]=0$ and $\Var Z=\Var(\ln X)$,
and under the hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{thm:martingale} it is a martingale difference.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$\E[\rho]=\E[\ln(N/X)]=\ln N-\E\ln X=H$ and $H_2=\ln N-\ln E$. Hence
$H-H_2=\ln\E[X]-\E[\ln X]\ge0$ by Jensen's inequality, with equality iff $X$ is
constant, which by \eqref{eq:pool-law} holds iff all class sizes are equal. The luck
term is $\rho-\E\rho$: mean zero, variance $\Var\rho=\Var(\ln X)$. The conditional
statement follows as in Theorem~\ref{thm:martingale}, applied to $\ln X$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Risk]
The risk term is the convexity gap between the average of the log and the log of the
average. An even split has $H=H_2$ and zero risk. One large class and several small
ones has a large gap: low expected reduction, high payoff if the answer is in a small
class. Take $100$ words in ten classes of ten, against one class of $91$ and nine
singletons. The first has $H_2=H=3.32$ bits and risk $0$. The second has skill
$0.27$, risk $0.45$ and expected reduction $0.72$ bits. Landing in a singleton (chance
$9\%$) reduces by $6.64$ bits $=0.27+0.45+5.92$. Landing in the $91$ (chance $91\%$)
reduces by $0.14=0.27+0.45-0.59$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Two luck scores]
$Z=\E[\ln X]-\ln A$ and the percentile luck $\Luck$ are both strictly decreasing in the
landed pool $A$ and both have mean zero, so they rank outcomes identically. $Z$ has the
units of skill and risk, which makes the accounting exact, and is bounded by
$\pm\ln N$. $\Luck$ is the mid-rank of the pool and is bounded by $\pm1$.
\end{remark}

\section{Several boards}\label{sec:multi}

Two variants hide several words. In a \emph{quartet} one guess is played on every
unsolved board at once. In a \emph{relay race} boards are played in turn, and every
earlier guess is applied to a board when it opens.

A guess is made for live boards $b=1,\dots,B$ with possible sets $\Omega_b$ of sizes
$N_b$. Skill $S_b$ and luck $\Luck_b$ are computed per board as above, with $E_b^\ast$
the best real guess for that board. Let $w_b=\log_2\max\{N_b,2\}$, which depends only on
the history, and
\[
S=\frac{\sum_b w_bS_b}{\sum_b w_b},\qquad
\Luck=\frac{\sum_b w_b\Luck_b}{\sum_b w_b}.
\]

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:multi}
$S\in[0,1]$ and $-1<\Luck<1$. If, given the history, each answer is uniform on its
board's candidates, then $\E[\Luck\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]=0$ and
Theorem~\ref{thm:martingale} holds for the combined luck. Independence between boards
is not required.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Convex combinations stay in $[0,1]$ and $(-1,1)$. The weights are
$\mathcal F_{t-1}$-measurable, so
$\E[\Luck\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]=\sum_b w_b\E[\Luck_b\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]/\sum_bw_b=0$
by Theorem~\ref{thm:luck}(3) on each board. The remainder of
Theorem~\ref{thm:martingale}'s proof uses only this.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Weights]
$\log_2N_b$ is the number of bits still to find on board $b$. A board with one word left
has weight one: playing that word is rewarded and missing it is penalised; its skill is
$1$ if the guess is that word and $0$ otherwise. Uniform weights would let a nearly
solved board dominate. The weights are a design choice.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Head start]
In a relay race a guess on the active board narrows boards not yet open. This credit is
not scored. It is reported: the \emph{head start} of a board is the number of words
possible on it when it opens, with all earlier guesses applied, against the size of the
list. An earlier guess can solve a board as it opens. Assigning this credit to the
earlier guesses is open.
\end{remark}

\section{Computation}\label{sec:computation}

Feedback for an $n$-letter guess is packed into an integer in $\{0,\dots,3^n-1\}$. For a
guess $g$, class sizes are counted in an array indexed by pattern, reset by revisiting
only the patterns touched. $E(g)$ costs one pass, $O(nN)$, with no allocation. Skill
needs $E^\ast=\min_gE(g)$, a scan of all $|\mathcal G|$ guesses: $O(nN|\mathcal G|)$,
about $1.2\times10^8$ operations for a first guess on $N=|\mathcal G|=4{,}994$
five-letter words, and fewer as $N$ falls. The scan also gives the skill of every
guess, so the rank of the player's guess is free. For several boards, boards sharing
the same candidate list (all of them at the start) are scored once. A truncated scan
gives $E^\ast$ too large and skill too high; the app scans every guess.

Luck needs the class sizes of the player's guess only: sort the distinct sizes and
accumulate the masses above and below, $O(k\log k)$ for $k$ classes.

\subsection{Is risk worth rewarding?}\label{sec:risk}

several small classes more, and counting groups more still. These are the R\'enyi
entropies of order $\alpha=\infty$ (the largest class), $2$ (the app), $1$ (Shannon) and
$0$ (number of classes). \texttt{risk\_attitude.py} plays a greedy player ranking guesses
by each. The first guess is chosen over every word; later guesses among the words still
possible and a sample of common words. The same $1{,}000$ uniformly drawn answers are
used for each (Table~\ref{tab:risk}).

\subsection{A worked game}\label{sec:game}

\texttt{worked\_game.py} follows one game: \textsc{caret}, then \textsc{least}, against
\textsc{agent}. After \textsc{caret} $18$ words remain. \textsc{least} splits them into
classes of $5,3,2,2,1,1,1,1,1,1$. Its expected pool is $E=2.67$. The best real guess is
\textsc{sepal}, with $E^\ast=1.56$ and classes $3,2,2$ and eleven singletons. \textsc{least}
scores $78$; \textsc{sepal} scores $100$.

The answer falls in the class of $5$ under \textsc{least}: reduction $1.85$ bits
$=2.75$ (skill) $+\,0.28$ (risk) $-\,1.19$ (luck), percentile luck $-72\%$. The best guess
\textsc{sepal} has skill $3.53$ and risk $0.15$ bits. \textsc{least} is a possible answer,
so it finishes the game at once with chance $1/18=5.6\%$; \textsc{sepal} is not a possible
answer and cannot. This is a gamble taken to finish in fewer guesses. It is priced in the
risk term ($0.28$ against $0.15$ bits), but only as a singleton class. The accounting
gives a win no more credit than any other singleton, although a win saves a whole guess
(Section~\ref{sec:open}).

\subsection{The game's answer draw}

Luck has mean zero when each possible word is equally likely. The game draws answers by
word frequency. \texttt{prior\_bias.py} gives the mean first-guess luck under that draw:
$+10.2\%$ for \textsc{tares}, $+10.6\%$ for \textsc{crane}, $+7.6\%$ for \textsc{slate},
$-6.4\%$ for \textsc{abbey}, $-8.2\%$ for \textsc{mamma}. Restricting the draw to the $500$
most common words changes these by about one point. Common words tend to fall in small
classes of strong guesses and large classes of weak ones. Under (U) the means are zero to
machine precision (Table~\ref{tab:luck}). This is the first open problem.

\subsection{A bound on the best conceivable split}

Three bounds on the number of groups were tested: the naive $\min(N,3^n)$; a bound $B$
counting $2$ outcomes at positions fixed by the clues and $3$ elsewhere; and a bound $C$
taking, over all real guesses, the product over letters of how many of
green/yellow/grey that letter could show against the words still possible. On $14$
simulated games ($40$ positions after $0$, $1$ or $2$ guesses), $B$ and $C$ equal the naive
bound in every position. Where $N>243$ the best scanned real guess made about $63\%$ of the
allowed groups. Where $N\le243$ a real guess separated every word in $3$ of $20$
positions. $C$ is not a valid bound for repeated letters ($7$ of $109{,}782$ guesses
exceeded it, all with a repeated letter). The bounds do not give a reachable best, so skill
is normalised by the best real guess.

\subsection{Numerical check of the theorems}

\texttt{verify\_theorems.py} checks Theorems~\ref{thm:luck} and~\ref{thm:martingale} and
Proposition~\ref{prop:alternatives} independently of the app. On $20{,}000$ random
partitions, luck lies strictly in $(-1,1)$, is strictly monotone in the pool, is unchanged
by scaling the classes, has $|\text{mean}|<2\times10^{-16}$, and has variance equal to
$(1-\sum q^3)/3$ to the same precision. On $1{,}500$ simulated games of four guesses, with
uniform answers and guesses depending only on the past, the mean luck of each guess is
within sampling error of zero (for the first guess $+0.008$, standard deviation $0.57$).
All correlations of a guess's luck with the skill of any guess so far, and with earlier
luck, are within four standard errors of zero (largest in magnitude $0.084$, tolerance
$0.103$).

\section{Open problems}\label{sec:open}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Non-uniform priors.} For frequency-weighted answers, replace sizes by prior
masses: $E=\sum_iW_i^2/W$ with $W_i$ the mass of class $i$. Theorems~\ref{thm:luck}
and~\ref{thm:martingale} hold with $p_i=W_i/W$, which would remove the bias of about
$\pm10$ points measured above. Open: whether the ranked pool is the word count or the
mass, and whether a player can be assumed to know the frequencies.
\item \textbf{Lookahead.} Skill scores the split made now. A guess can score lower and
play better over the rest of the game. Is there a skill that credits lookahead and
depends only on the player's knowledge, for example through the expected number of
further guesses of an optimal continuation \cite{bertsimas2022}?
\item \textbf{Scale of skill.} The logarithm of the expected pool is used because the
linear scale compresses first guesses near $100$. Other R\'enyi orders, or a scale
calibrated to the guesses a strong player needs, are alternatives. Test against how strong
players rank openings.
\item \textbf{Risk attitude and tries left.} The best entropy order should depend on the
guesses remaining: an even split with many left, a gamble with few (Section~\ref{sec:risk}).
Is there a skill that depends on the tries left, for example the chance of finishing within
them under an optimal continuation?
\item \textbf{Credit for finishing now.} A guess that is a possible answer wins at
once with chance $1/N$. In the accounting the winning class is a singleton like any
other, with reduction $\ln N$. But landing in another singleton still costs one more
guess; landing in the winning class does not. Skill and risk therefore undercount
guesses that can win. A measure based on the expected number of remaining guesses would
count it. How should this be combined with the entropy terms?
\item \textbf{Total luck of a game.} Is there a normalised total luck comparable across
games of different length, for example $\sum_t\Luck_t/\sqrt{\sum_t\Var\Luck_t}$?
\item \textbf{Relay-race credit.} How to credit the head start of a board to the earlier
guesses that produced it.
\item \textbf{Ties.} Mid-ranks are used for equal pools. Strict ranks give a luck with
nonzero mean. Other tie rules exist. Does fairness characterise the mid-rank rule?
\item \textbf{Hard modes, other feedback.} Only the following is used about $f$: a map to
a finite set with all-green exactly at equality. Do the results and the algorithm extend to
games where later guesses must obey earlier clues, and how does $E^\ast$ change?
\item \textbf{Approximating $E^\ast$.} The exact scan is quadratic in the list size.
Are there provable shortcuts, such as a lower bound on $E^\ast$ from letter statistics of
$\Omega$, for very large lists or scripts with more patterns?
\end{enumerate}
